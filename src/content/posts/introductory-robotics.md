# Introductory Robotics

This page is a collection of the robot designs, CAD experiments, and early engineering ideas that helped me start taking hardware seriously.

A lot of these designs are not finished robots. Some are concept sketches, some are mechanisms I never fabricated, and some are early attempts at understanding how a robot becomes a system instead of just a shape in CAD. That is part of why I want to keep them here.

## Where I started

My early robotics work was a way to learn by making visible things. I could draw a chassis, place wheels, imagine how a mechanism might move, and then slowly discover all the constraints I had not considered yet: clearances, fasteners, center of mass, motor placement, wiring, assembly order, and whether the thing could actually be serviced after it was built.

That process was humbling, but useful. CAD gave me a way to ask better questions before spending time or money on fabrication.

### Miscellaneous chassis studies

**What I was exploring:** wheel placement, chassis proportions, mechanical packaging, and the visual language of a robot that might actually be buildable.

**What I learned:** a design can look convincing while still having no realistic assembly plan. The boring details are often the engineering details.

### Battlebot concepts

**What I was exploring:** armor, weapon placement, drive layouts, and how mechanical choices change the strategy of a small combat robot.

**What I learned:** designing for a competition means designing around failure, maintenance, and constraints. A clever mechanism is only useful if it can survive testing and be repaired.

### Micromouse beginnings

**What I was exploring:** compact electronics packaging, sensor placement, motor control, and the relationship between a PCB and the chassis around it.

**What I learned:** this was where I started to understand that embedded systems are physical systems. The schematic, PCB, firmware, sensors, motors, and chassis all have to agree.

## Looking back

The biggest value of these early designs is not that they were completed. It is that they gave me a place to practice thinking in systems.

I learned to ask:

- What is the next thing I need to prove?
- Can I assemble and repair this design?
- Where do the electronics, mechanics, and software meet?
- What did I assume without testing?
- What would I change in the next revision?

Those questions are still present in my current work. My newer projects are more focused on embedded systems, telemetry, flight electronics, and sensing, but the habit began with drawing robot parts and trying to imagine how they would become a working machine.

## What comes next

I want to revisit this archive as I design a new omniwheel micromouse and connect it to a ground control system. That project would let me bring together the early robotics work with the newer lessons I am learning from flight computers, telemetry, and system-level validation.

The next version should not just be a better robot. It should be a better experiment.
